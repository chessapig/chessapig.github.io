## Math

You can see a lot of math with the normal cloth. Let's check the normal vectors of a plane:
<div class="card my-4 shadow-sm" style="max-width: 600px; margin: auto;">
  <img src="/assets/ihp/normals/plane.jpeg" alt="Normal cloth sitting on the table, all the vectors pointing rigth up" class="card-img-top" >

  <div class="card-body text-center">
    <p class="card-text"> Normal vectors of a plane
    </p>
  </div>
</div>
Like we expected, they're all pointing the same direction! Well, kinda.  The straws are a little bendy, so there's like 5 degree random variation in each. It looks like a bump map, a trick that 3D artists use to make surfaces seem bumpy. I'd like a version with less variation, but I don't know how to make it. When the surface isn't so flat, the deviation from the platonic realm isn't so obvious. Now let's put it on the hyperbolic plane!

<div class="card my-4 shadow-sm" style="max-width: 600px; margin: auto;">
  <img src="/assets/ihp/normals/top.jpeg" alt="Normal cloth draped over a saddle" class="card-img-top" >

  <div class="card-body text-center">
    <p class="card-text"> Normal vectors of a saddle
    </p>
  </div>
</div>
This is shot from from the top down This picture looks like a vector field in the plane, with a zero at the center of the image where the normal is pointing towards us. The linear approximation to this vector field at zero is given by a matrix, which is the second fundamental form. And we can see it with the normals! I love how obvious this makes the directions of principle curvature, which are the eigenvectors of the second fundamental form. In this picture, they're vertical and horizontal. 

Here's the same surface, but looking along one of the diagonals 
<div class="card my-4 shadow-sm" style="max-width: 600px; margin: auto;">
  <img src="/assets/ihp/normals/asymptotic.jpeg" alt="Normal cloth draped over a saddle, looked along a diagonal. It s like a forest, with the trees tilting as you move through" class="card-img-top" >

  <div class="card-body text-center">
    <p class="card-text"> Close up of the normal vectors
    </p>
  </div>
</div>
Notice how the normals twist as you move along the surface. This is one of the hallmarks of negative curvature. The rate of the twisting is related to the curvature of the surface. This property is essential to my [mechanical model of a constant negative curvature surface](/ihp/wunderlich)
## Construction
This was made by 3D printing through fabric. I bought some stretchy tulle fabric from the most insane fabric store I've ever seen. 
<div class="card my-4 shadow-sm" style="max-width: 400px; margin: auto;">
  <img src="/assets/ihp/normals/store.jpeg" alt="An extremly cramped store full of fabric floor to ceiling." class="card-img-top" >

  <div class="card-body text-center">
    <p class="card-text"> Ah, paris 
    </p>
  </div>
</div>
You 3D print a few layers, then pause the printer. Tape the fabric on top, and resume. The PLA melts through the holes in the tulle. This was the first 3D print I ever made, and it worked really well. Here is a [guide](https://www.instructables.com/How-to-3D-Print-Onto-Fabric/). Many thanks with Henry Segerman for designing the model. Here is the [STL file](/assets/ihp/normals/hexagon_file.stl), if you'd like to print it yourself.  
<div class="card my-4 shadow-sm" style="max-width: 600px; margin: auto;">
  <img src="/assets/ihp/normals/printing.jpeg" alt="a 3D printer mid print with fabric taped to the print bed." class="card-img-top" >

  <div class="card-body text-center">
    <p class="card-text"> the normal vectors mid 3D print. 
    </p>
  </div>
</div>
Each puck has a hole in it for an M3 screw, with a recess so that the head of the screw is flush with the bottom. After printing, I poke screws through all the holes. Then I add my normal vectors in the form of plastic straws. I used the same straws from [this sculpture](/ihp/straws), which coincidentally were exactly M3 sized. The straws screwed on, held tight by the threads of the screws and acting as a nut.
