# Background
## The Mahler conjecture
Every math field comes with its very own duality. The originator is linear algebra, where a vector space $V$ is dual to the space of one-forms $V^\ast$. This manifests in Euclidean geometry as a duality between points and half-spaces. Each one-form $\alpha$ defines a half-space containing the origin, $H_\alpha = \lbrace v  \in V \, \big \vert\,  \alpha(v) \leq 1\rbrace$. In polyhedral geometry, a convex polyhedron can be described as either the convex hull of its vertices, or as the intersection of half-spaces corresponding to its facets. The vertex and facet descriptions are dual. 

The fundamental duality in convex geometry is *polar duality*. For a convex body $K \subset V$ define the polar $K^\circ \subset V^\ast$  as the collection of half-spaces containing $K$. That is,

$$K = \{ \alpha \in V^\ast \, \big\vert \, K^\circ \subset H_\alpha\}$$

The convex body $K$ is thought of as a set of points, while its polar $K^\circ$ is a collection of hyperplanes. Identifying $V$ with $V^\ast$ using the Euclidean metric, we can compare pictures. Play around with these in 2D to get a feel. Try making $K$ long and skinny, and see what happens to $K^\circ$.

> [!sketch] mahler conjecture/polar polygon
> A symmetric polygon $K$ and its polar $K^\circ$. The defining half-spaces of $K$ correspond to the vertices of $K^\circ$. 

The polar operation behaves nicely. 
- $K^\circ \subset V^\ast$ is convex. If $K$ is closed, symmetric and convex, then $K^{\circ\circ} = K$.   The polar is an honest duality relation.
- $(\lambda K)^\circ = \frac{1}{\lambda}K^\circ$. Scaling up the body scales down its polar.

Exploiting the scaling property, we can build a scale-invariant quantity from $K$ and its polar. Let $\vert K\vert$ denote the volume of $K$. Define the *Mahler volume* as the product of volumes of $K$ and its polar:

$$M(K) = \vert K\vert\cdot \vert K^\circ\vert $$

The largest possible Mahler volume is achieved by a ball, $M(K) \leq M(B(1))$. The minimal Mahler volume was, until now, unknown.

> [!conjecture] Mahler conjecture
> Suppose $K$ is symmetric, meaning $K = -K$.  The minimal Mahler volume is achieved by a cube.
> 
> $$M(K) \geq M([-1,1]^n) = \frac{4^n}{n!} $$

We can think of Mahler volume as a measure of roundness. The (unique) highest roundness shape is a ball, the (non-unique) lowest roundness symmetric shape is a cube. For more details, history, and intuition, see [Terry Tao's blog](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2007/03/08/open-problem-the-mahler-conjecture-on-convex-bodies/).  This problem has been kicking around for at least 100 years, and has inspired some [beautiful bridges](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-29849-3_20) between convex geometry and several complex variables. It also ties closely with symplectic geometry.

## Symplectic capacities and the Viterbo conjecture
Let us combine $K$ and $K^\circ$ into a single object, their Cartesian product $K \times K^\circ \subset V \times V^\ast$.  We will be making a lot of pictures of this space, and now is a good time to set the visual language. We will always draw two dimensional bodies $K$, so $K\times K^\circ$ is a four dimensional shape. The two main ways to render a 4 dimensional object is through projection or slicing. Here's the projection onto the screen, so you can look around. 

> [!sketch] mahler conjecture/polar product
> On the left, The product $K\times K^\circ$, projected from $V\times V^\ast$ to the screen. In the middle is the view selector. place the red selectors in the middle to long along the red dimension, and vice versa for the green selectors.  On the right, a selector for the polygon $K$ and its dual $K^\circ$.

A lot going on, right? Slicing will be more understandable for us. For any shape $\Omega \subset V\times V^\ast$, denote the slice above a point $x\in V$ as $\Omega^x = \Omega \cap \lbrace x \rbrace \times V^\ast$. By stacking all the slices $\Omega^x$ next to each other, we reconstruct $\Omega$. When $V$ is two dimensional, we can draw $\Omega$! Let the screen represent $V$. For a representative array of points on $V$, draw a scaled down copy of $\Omega^x$. Lining these up next to one another, our eyes notice the changes in the slices for each $x$. Applying this technique to $\Omega = K \times K^\circ$ is not so exciting, since every slice is the polar $\Omega^x = K^\circ$. Later on, we will see the slices change with position.

> [!sketch|square] mahler conjecture/hexagonal grid/polar.html
> Visualization of $K\times K^\circ$. You are invited to think of $K$ as an island, and the small copies of $K^\circ$ as flowers populating the island. This visual metaphor is inspired by [geography and botany](https://chessapig.github.io/talks/Big-Small).


Geometrically, the ambient space $V \times V^\ast$ is a cotangent bundle $T^\ast V$, which carries a natural symplectic structure (i.e nondegenerate 2 form). In coordinates, if $V$ has coordinates $q_i$ and $V^\ast$ has dual coordinates $p_i$, then $\omega_{std} = \sum \de q_i \wedge \de p_i$ is the symplectic form. This makes $K\times K^\circ$ into a symplectic domain, whose symplectic volume equals the Mahler volume. Motivated by the Mahler conjecture, is there a uniform lower bound for the symplectic size of $K\times K^\circ$?



We formalize the notion of symplectic size with a symplectic capacity.  Consider a machine $c$ which takes a symplectic domain $(D,\omega)$ and outputs a real number $c(D,\omega)$ (we will often use $c(D)$ as shorthand when the symplectic structure is implicit). In order for $c$ to represent symplectic size, it must satisfy a few axioms. First, it must be symplectic.
1. **Symplectic invariance:** for any diffeomorphism $\psi:D \to D'$, we have $c(D',\omega) = c(D,\psi^\ast\omega)$. 

Second, it must be a "size". For any good notion of size, if a domain $D$ fits inside of $D'$, then the size of $D$ is less than or equal to that of $D'$. In the symplectic world, to fit is to symplectically embed. A map $\psi:(D,\omega) \hookrightarrow (D',\omega')$ is a symplectic embedding if it is an embedding satisfying $\psi^\ast \omega' = \omega$. This motivates our second axiom.
2. **Monotonicity:** If there exists a symplectic embedding $\psi:D \hookrightarrow D'$, then $c(D) \leq c(D')$

Anything satisfying $1$ and $2$ is a symplectic measure of size. For our sanity, we demand that the size scales linearly with the symplectic form. 
3. **Scaling:** $c(D,\lambda \omega) = \lambda c(D,\omega)$. 

A machine $c$ satisfying axioms $1-3$ is known as a *symplectic capacity*. 

The quintessential capacity is the Gromov width $c_{Gr}$, which measures the largest ball that symplectically embeds inside the domain. To fix notation, we define some standard symplectic domains. Pick coordinates $q_i$ on $V$, and dual coordinates $p_i$ on $V^\ast$.  Define the ball $B^n(A)$ and a cylinder $Z^n(A)$, which are symplectic domains endowed with the standard symplectic form

$$\begin{align}
B^n(A) &= \{ \pi \sum\limits_{i=1}^n(\vert q_i\vert^2 + \vert p_i\vert^2) < A\}\\ 
Z^n(A) &= \{ \pi (\vert q_1\vert^2 + \vert p_1\vert^2) < A\}
\end{align}$$

These are parametrized by their two dimensional cross-sectional area $A$. Define the *Gromov width*:

$$c_{Gr}(D) = \sup\{ A\, \vert \exists \text{ symplectic embedding }\Psi:B^n(A) \hookrightarrow D \}$$

For example, Gromov's non-squeezing theorem states that $c_{Gr}(Z^n(A))=A$, meaning a ball cannot symplectically embed into a cylinder with smaller cross-sectional area. You can't squeeze a ball into a smaller tube.

A bold conjecture from 2000 posits that many measures of symplectic sizes agree on symmetric convex bodies. Say that a symplectic capacity is *normalized* if $c(B^n(A)) = c(Z^n(A))$. For example, the Gromov width is normalized. 

> [!conjecture] Strong Viterbo conjecture
> Let $c,c'$ be normalized symplectic capacities. Then for any symmetric convex body $\Omega \subset T^\ast V$, the capacities agree
> 
> $$c(\Omega) = c'(\Omega)$$

\[AAKO\][^4] showed that the Mahler conjecture is a corollary of the Viterbo conjecture. Specifically, they proved that the Hofer–Zehnder capacity $c_{HZ}$, another normalized capacity, satisfies $c_{HZ}(K \times K^\circ) = 4$. Viterbo's conjecture implies

$$\text{Viterbo's conjecture }\implies c_{Gr}(K\times K^\circ) = c_{HZ}(K \times K^\circ)=4$$

That is, there is a symplectic embedding of a ball of width $4$ into $K \times K^\circ$. Since symplectic maps preserve volume, this gives a lower bound on volume. 

$$c_{Gr}(K \times K^\circ) \geq 4 \implies \Vol(K\times K^\circ) \geq \Vol(B^n(4)) = \frac{4^n}{n!}$$

matching the lower bound conjectured by Mahler! This paper was fairly influential, and spurred renewed interest in Viterbo's conjecture, which continues to be a driving problem in symplectic geometry (until the next AI drop...)

The result du jour realizes this story:
> [!theorem] AI
>  The Gromov width of $K \times K^\circ$ equals $4$. 

^thm-AI

This does not go through any sort of Viterbo conjecture, though it does imply the Viterbo conjecture for $K\times K^\circ$.  Perhaps the techniques could be strengthened to the full Viterbo conjecture. 

The AI proof explicitly constructs a symplectic embedding of a ball $B^n(4)$ into $K\times K^\circ$, supplying a lower bound $c_{Gr}(K\times K^\circ)\geq 4$. Combined with the upper bound $c_{Gr}(K \times K^\circ) \leq 4$ on the last page of \[AAKO\][^4], this gives the result. The AI seems very capable at creating crazy symplectic embeddings, evidenced by their construction of [high dimensional ball packing](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/Symplectic-Ball-Packings-in-Higher-Dimensions-September-23-2026/paper.pdf).

# Proof

I will present the proof as if it were written by a colleague, assuming it's correct and outlining the ideas. I haven't checked the line-by-line details (which is important, because this paper doesn't come with a Lean proof).  The proof has two parts. First, it embeds a symplectic ball in the intersection of a holomorphic submanifold with a ball. Second, it constructs a sequence of holomorphic submanifolds which, when intersected with a ball, is a subset of $K \times K^\circ$. Stated as theorems,  

> [!theorem] Symplectic monotonicity formula (section 2)
> Let $i: M\hookrightarrow \CC^N$ be a smooth holomorphic submanifold passing through the origin, endowed with the pullback of the standard symplectic form $\omega = i^\ast\omega_{std}$. Consider the intersection of $M$ with a ball of area $\pi \lambda$ centered at the origin,  $M^{<\lambda} = M\cap B^N(\pi \lambda)$. The Gromov width of $M^{<\lambda}$ is bounded from below by that of the ball
> 
> $$c_{Gr}(M^{<\lambda},i^\ast\omega) \geq \pi \lambda.$$
> 
> If $M$ intersects zero with multiplicity $k$, then for a desingularizing symplectic form $\omega_0$,
> 
> $$c_{Gr}(M^{<\lambda},i^\ast\omega + \omega_0) \geq \pi k\lambda.$$ 

^thm-monotonicity

The first part of this theorem is going to be a central result in my upcoming paper, so I guess I got scooped. The rest of the proof is a clever construction of the holomorphic submanifold.  A convex body $K$ is a limit of symmetric polyhedra (section 5), which are the intersection of symmetric strips. From now on, assume $K$ is the intersection of $N$ strips defined by vectors $b_i$, meaning

$$K  = \bigcap_{i=1}^N\{\langle x,b_i\rangle\vert\leq 1\}$$

> [!theorem] Holomorphic submanifold construction (section 4)
>  Define $\ell_i(z) = \langle z,b_i\rangle$ to be the complexified linear functional determined by each strip. For a holomorphic function $g:\CC\to \CC$, define a holomorphic map $f_k:\CC^n \to \CC^N$ by
>  
>  $$f_k(z) = (g(\ell_1(z))^k , \dots, g(\ell_N(z))^k)$$
>  
> and denote its image by $M_k = f_k(\CC^n)$. The intersection with the unit ball $(M_k^{< 1},f_k^\ast\omega_{std})$ is symplectomorphic to a domain in the symplectic vector space $(D_k,\omega_{std}) \subset \CC^n$. There exists a function $g$ such that as $k\to \infty$, the domain $D_k$ is a subset of $K \times \frac{\pi k}{4} K^\circ$. 
> 
> Explicitly, $g$ is a conformal transform from a lens-shaped domain $L\subset \CC$ to the unit disc $\mathbb{D}$ such that the angular coordinate on $\del \mathbb{D}$ pulls back to the $x$-coordinate on $\del L$ (Constructed by [Gross](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.00852v1) in the context of Brownian motion).
> 
> > [!figure|600] Key conformal map
> > ![](sketch/mahler conjecture/images/map.jpg)
> > Drawing of the lens domain $L$ and the conformal map to $\mathbb{D}$. 

^thm-holomorphic

Combining these two theorems gives the main result. The holomorphic map $f_k$ has a zero of order $k$, so $c_{Gr}(M_k^{< 1}) \geq k\pi$. Therefore, applying monotonicity in the limit $k\to \infty$, 

$$k \pi \leq c_{Gr}(K \times \frac{\pi k}{4} K^\circ)= \frac{k \pi}{4} c_{Gr}(K \times K^\circ)$$

This obtains the desired lower bound on Gromov width $4 \leq c_{Gr}(K\times K^\circ)$.  \[AAKO\][^4] construct a symplectic embedding of $K\times K^\circ$ into a cylinder $Z^n(4)$, in remark 4.2. By Gromov non-squeezing, this gives an upper bound  $c_{Gr}(K\times K^\circ)\leq 4$. All together, this proves [[#^thm-AI]].

$$\boxed{c_{Gr}(K\times K^\circ) = 4}$$

If you don't care about the symplectic geometry, you can skip the discussion of the symplectic monotonicity formula.  Replacing the symplectic monotonicity formula with the more elementary Lelong principle from complex geometry (recalled below) gives the bound $\Vol(K\times K^\circ) \geq \text{Vol}(B^n(4))$, which is the Mahler conjecture. This is the core of the proof in [symmetric Mahler conjecture paper](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/The-symmetric-Mahler-conjecture-and-its-equality-cases-September-22-2026/paper.pdf).

The motivation for the holomorphic submanifold construction in the AI paper is opaque. I hope to give an alternate universe story, explaining how a human might have come to this example, and find the magic conformal transform $g$. 

## Symplectic monotonicity theorem
In this section we will prove [[#^thm-monotonicity]]. This one was easy for me to interpret because I Already Wrote The Proof. (This isn't a plagiarism situation, since I never fed the proof into ChatGPT. Still sucks though). I'll give a more complete account in my upcoming paper. 

This result is a sharpening of the monotonicity lemma from complex geometry, familiar from its application to $J$-curves in the proof of Gromov's non-squeezing theorem. For a holomorphic submanifold $M \subset \CC^n$, the monotonicity lemma bounds the growth rate of $\Vol(M^{<\lambda})$. Here are three related results:
1. $\Vol(M^{<\lambda})$ grows at least as quickly as a ball of the same radius in the same dimension. That is,

   $$\frac{\Vol(M^{<\lambda})}{\Vol(B^n(\pi \lambda))} \text{ is monotonically increasing in }\lambda$$

If we understand the ratio of volumes near $\lambda=0$, we can extract an absolute bound on the volume of the intersection with the ball. Here are a couple of manifestations:
2. If $M$ passes through the origin, then $\Vol(M^{<\lambda})\geq \Vol(B^n(\pi \lambda))$.
3. (Lelong principle) If $M$ is a variety that intersects the origin with multiplicity $k$, then $\Vol(M^{<\lambda})\geq k\Vol(B^n(\pi \lambda))$

We upgrade each of these into symplectic statements, realizing the volume growth condition by symplectic embeddings. The explicit volume bounds are realized by constructing symplectic balls.  This is an elaboration of [[#^thm-monotonicity]]:
> [!theorem] Symplectic monotonicity 
>Endow the submanifold $i:M\hookrightarrow (\CC^N,\omega_{std})$ with the pullback of the standard symplectic form $\omega = i^\ast\omega_{std}$
>1.  There exists a symplectic embedding
>
 >    $$\left(M^{<\lambda}, \frac{\Lambda}{\lambda} \omega\right) \hookrightarrow \left(M^{<\Lambda},\omega\right)$$
>
>1.  If $M$ passes through the origin, the Gromov width of the intersection with the ball is bounded from below as $c_{Gr}(M^{<\Lambda})\geq \pi \Lambda$. That is, there exists a symplectic embedding
>
 >    $$(B^{n}(\pi \Lambda),\omega_{std}) \hookrightarrow (M^{<\Lambda},\omega)$$
>
>2. Suppose $M$ intersects the origin with multiplicity $k$. The Gromov width of the intersection with the ball, with symplectic form perturbed by $\omega_0$, is bounded from below as
>   
>     $$c_{Gr}(M^{<\Lambda}, \omega + \omega_0)\geq \pi k \Lambda.$$

^thm-monotonicity-long


Part 3 above is the content of theorem 2.1 of the AI paper, for a particular choice of $\omega_0$. When the multiplicity of intersection with $0$ is more than 1, then $i^\ast\omega$ is degenerate at zero. The perturbing form $\omega_0$ removes this degeneracy, and we should think of $\omega_0$ as being small. For simplicity of exposition, we will take $\omega_0=0$ and ignore the degeneracy of $\omega$.

The proof relies on a few fundamental computations. Denote the complex structure on $M$ by $J$. For a function $u$, define $\de^cu = - \de u \circ J$. If $\varphi$  is a holomorphic map, then $\varphi^\ast \de^c u = \de^c \varphi^\ast u$. In particular, observe that $\omega_{std} = \frac{1}{4}\de \de^c \vert z\vert^2$. Since $i:M\to \CC^N$ is holomorphic,

  $$\omega =  \frac{1}{4}i^\ast \de \de^c \vert z\vert^2 =  \de \de^c  \frac{1}{4} i^\ast \vert z\vert^2$$


  The Kähler potential of the pullback is $\rho =  \frac{1}{4}i^\ast\vert z\vert^2$ . Now we can think of $M^{<\lambda}$ as a sublevel set$\rho\inv[0,\frac{1}{4}\lambda)$. 

> [!lemma] 
> For $\omega = \de \de^c \rho$,  introduce the metric compatible with $J$ and $\omega$. The gradient with respect to this metric $\nabla \rho$ is Liouville, meaning $\mathcal{L}_{\nabla \rho}\omega = \omega$. 

>[!proof]-
> The defining relation of the gradient is that $\omega(\nabla \rho,J\cdot)=\de \rho$. Move $J$ to the other side, $\omega(\nabla \rho,\cdot) = -d \rho \circ J = \de^c \rho$. Apply Cartan's magic formula
> 
> $$\calL_{\nabla \rho} \omega = \de i_{\nabla \rho}\omega = \de \de^c \rho = \omega$$

The symplectic embeddings of the theorem are constructed by flowing by the gradient of the Kähler potential $\nabla \rho$. This expands the level sets of $\rho$ into one another while scaling up the symplectic form. The following key estimate controls how fast the gradient flow moves between level sets:

> [!lemma]  Key estimate
> Suppose $\rho = \frac{1}{4} i^\ast \vert z\vert^2$. Then, $\vert\nabla \rho\vert^2 \leq \rho$. 

^lemma-key-estimate

 >[!proof]+
 >Since $M$ is a holomorphic submanifold, the metric is the pullback of the Riemannian metric on $\CC^n$. Denoting $\nabla_{std}$ for the gradient of a function on $\CC^n$, the internal gradient $\nabla i^\ast \vert z\vert^2$ is the orthogonal projection of the external gradient $\nabla_{std}\vert z\vert^2$ onto the tangent space of $M$. Orthogonal projection reduces norm, so in their respective metrics,
 >
 >
 >$$\vert\nabla \frac{1}{4} i^\ast \vert z\vert^2\vert^2 \leq \vert \frac{1}{4} \nabla_{std} \vert z\vert^2\vert^2 = \frac{1}{4}\vert z\vert^2$$
 >
 >
 >Pulling back to $M$ gives the desired inequality $\vert\nabla \rho\vert^2 \leq \rho$. 

Note that this fact is equivalent to the fact that $\log(\rho)$ is PSH, which is the property used in the AI paper in the proof of theorem 2.1. 
### Proof of [[#^thm-monotonicity-long]]
In the following, fix $i:M \hookrightarrow \CC^N$ and define the Kähler potential $\rho = \frac{1}{4} i^\ast\vert z\vert^2$, with $\omega = \de \de^c \rho$.

**Part 1:** We will construct symplectic embeddings between sublevel sets $M^{< \lambda } = \rho\inv[0,\frac{\lambda}{4})$. Define $\phi_t$ as the flow of $\nabla \rho$, which is an expanding flow.  Since $\nabla \rho$ is Liouville, $\phi_t^\ast \omega=  e^{t} \omega$. This gives a symplectomorphism between domains $(M^{<\lambda},\omega) \cong (\phi_t(M^{<\lambda}), e^{-t}\omega)$. Now we find a larger level set containing $\phi_t(M^{<\lambda})$. Let $\gamma$ be a flowline of $\nabla \rho$. Applying  [[#^lemma-key-estimate]], we obtain $\del_t \rho(\gamma(t))  = \vert\nabla \rho\vert^2 \leq \rho(\gamma(t))$.  Integrating the differential inequality and applying the comparison theorem for ODEs, 

$$\rho(\gamma(t)) \leq e^t\rho(\gamma(0))$$

This ensures an inclusion of the flowed level set $\phi_t(M^{<\lambda}) \subset M^{<e^t \lambda}$.  We have thus produced a symplectic embedding $\phi_t:(M^{<\lambda},\omega) \hookrightarrow (M^{< e^t \lambda}, e^{-t}\omega)$. Writing $\Lambda = e^t \lambda$ and scaling both sides by $e^t$ gives the desired relation

$$(M^{<\lambda}, \frac{\Lambda}{\lambda}\omega) \hookrightarrow (M^{<\Lambda}, \omega)$$

**Part 2** is a consequence of part 1, taking the limit $\lambda \to 0$.  By monotonicity and scaling properties of the Gromov width, $c_{Gr}(M^{<\Lambda}) \geq \frac{\Lambda}{\lambda} c_{Gr}(M^{<\lambda})$. Define a function $c(\lambda) = c_{Gr}(M^{<\lambda})$ which records the Gromov width as a function of $\lambda$. Taking the limit as $\lambda \to 0$ , the above inequality gives a lower bound of the Gromov width of $M^{<\Lambda}$ in terms of the derivative of $c$

$$c_{Gr}(M^{<\Lambda}) \geq \Lambda c'(0)$$

Now we compare to a local form. If the minimum of $\rho$ is not zero, then $c'(0)=0$ and we get no lower bound. We only get a meaningful bound when $\rho\inv(0)$ is nonempty, which is the case when $M$ passes through the origin.  Assume that the origin is a regular point of $M$. Then, $M^{<\lambda}$ is approximately a complex plane passing through zero when $\lambda$ is small, so $(M^{<\lambda},i^\ast\omega_{std}) \approx B^n(\pi \lambda)$. Thus,

$$\del_\lambda c_{Gr}(M^{<\lambda}) =\del_\lambda c_{Gr}(B^n(\pi \lambda)) = \pi $$

proving $c_{Gr}(M^{<\Lambda}) \geq \pi \Lambda$, and part 2. 

**Part 3** requires some care. As mentioned, I will ignore technicalities about the degeneracy of $\omega$ and give a heuristic. Following part 2, we need to examine the Gromov width of sublevel sets of a local model. If $M$ intersects the origin with multiplicity $k$, then a local model near the origin looks like $\rho = \frac{1}{4}\vert z\vert^{2k}$. The form $\omega = \frac{1}{4}\de \de^c \vert z\vert^{2k}$ (though degenerate) is symplectomorphic outside of the origin to the standard symplectic form by a radial diffeomorphism, which by standard manipulations satisfies the formula $\del_\lambda c_{Gr}(M^{<\lambda}) = k \pi$. Applying the argument from part 2, we get $c_{Gr}(M^{<\lambda}) \geq k \pi \lambda$. Here is a proof sketch.
>[!proof]-
> The existence of the radial diffeomorphism follows by matching up the Liouville flows of $\nabla \rho$ with those of $\vert z\vert^2$, see McDuff [^1].  Explicitly, if $\omega_{std} = \frac{1}{4}\de \de^c \vert z\vert^2$, and $\omega = \frac{1}{4}\de \de^c f(\vert z\vert^2)$, then the rescaling is given by
> 
> $$R(z) = \sqrt{f'(\vert z\vert^2)}z \qquad R^\ast \omega_{std} = \omega$$
> 
> See, for example, equation (23) of Loi and Zuddas[^2]. Applying this to $f(s) =s^k$, the sublevel set $\vert z\vert^{2k}< \lambda$ is symplectomorphic to the standard ball of radius
> 
> $$R\left(\lambda^{1/{2k}}\right) = \sqrt{k \lambda^{(k-1)/k}}\lambda^{1/2k} = \sqrt{k \lambda}$$ 
> 
> thus the Gromov width of $\rho\inv[0,\frac{\lambda}{4})$ relative to the form $\frac{1}{4}\de \de^c \vert z\vert^{2k}$ is $k \pi \lambda$. Plugging this into the derivative formula, we achieve the absolute bound $c_{Gr}(M^{<\lambda}) \geq k \pi \lambda$.  (I haven't tried to make this heuristic precise. I imagine it will come out similar to the AI proof).

### Visualizing the symplectic balls 

The symplectic balls are obtained by placing a very small ball near zero, then flowing by $\nabla \rho$ for a long time. I think of this as the "pancake syrup" map. Place a dollop of syrup at the top of your pancakes, stacked like $-\rho$, and watch it spread out. If you [like your math big](https://chessapig.github.io/talks/Big-Small), then it's like a viscous lava spreading from the peak of a steep volcano. 

To visualize the relation of the pancake syrup map with the embedding, we can move to real submanifolds $M\subset \RR^3$. We play the same game, defining the function $\rho = \frac{1}{4}\vert x\vert^2:M \to \RR$  and considering the gradient flow of $\rho$ relative to the induced metric. We still have the key identity $\vert\nabla \rho\vert^2\leq \rho$, so the arguments from before hold. After flowing for time $t$, the sublevel set $M^{<\lambda}$ is included in $M^{<e^t \lambda}$. How does this inclusion look? Below, we show the image of an infinitesimal level set $M^{<\varepsilon}$, flowed until it no longer necessarily fits into $M^{<r^2}$ where you can control $r$. The left shows the result on $M$, and the right shows the graph of $1-\vert x \vert^2$, parametrized according to the exponential map in the induced metric.

As we expect, the ball grows slower than the level set. Intrinsically, the ball grows most slowly when $\nabla \rho$ is small, and matches with the level set when $\vert \nabla \rho\vert ^2 = \rho$.  Any variations in $\vert \nabla \rho\vert$ are accentuated. For example, the island above has 4 shallow flanks and 4 steeper valleys. The lava flows fastest down the valleys, where moving quicker past regions where the level sets are spaced close together. This implies the image of the ball is the opposite of the shape of the level set, spiky when the level set is flat and vice versa. We don't expect any ball filling to be close to sharp, unless $\vert \nabla \rho\vert ^2 = \rho$ everywhere. 

 Extrinsically, you can see the ball falls away from the level set when the submanifold curves away from the radial vector. Indeed, sharpness of the ball embedding requires that the orthogonal projection of $\nabla \vert x\vert ^2$ is equal to $\nabla \vert x\vert^2$, or that $M$ is everywhere tangent to the radial line. To get sharp balls, $M$ must be a cone. The fact that AI used this technique to produce a full filling (in the case of a cube) means the constructed submanifold must be a cone. 
## Constructing the holomorphic submanifold
With symplectic monotonicity in hand, we have a flexible way to construct high dimensional ball embeddings. The challenge is now to produce a holomorphic submanifold whose intersection with the unit ball is symplectomorphic to $K \times K^\circ$ ([[#^thm-holomorphic]]). The approach is to approximate $K$ with symmetric polyhedra, which raises the first conceptual hurdle. We expect the intersection of a submanifold with the unit ball to be smooth — how can it fill out the corners of $K\times K^\circ$?

### Limiting with $L^\infty$ norms
Let's warm up in the real setting. We describe a sequence of real submanifolds $\ell^k:\RR^n \hookrightarrow \RR^N$ whose intersection with a unit ball limits to a convex body $K\subset \RR^n$. Define the convex body as an intersection of strips. 

$$K  = \bigcap_{i=1}^N\{ \vert\langle x,b_i\rangle\vert\leq 1\}$$

Let $\ell_i(x)=  \langle x,b_i\rangle$ be the defining linear functions. Rewrite $K$ as a sublevel set of the maximum of defining functions, $K = \{ x\in \RR^n \vert \max_{i} \vert\ell_i(x)\vert\leq 1\}$. The set of $\ell_i$ defines a linear embedding

$$\ell:\RR^n \to \RR^N, \quad\ell(x) = (\ell_1(x),\dots,\ell_N(x))$$

Geometrically, the convex body $K$ is the intersection of this embedding with the unit hypercube, $K = \ell(\RR^n)\cap [-1,1]^N$. Analytically, $K$ is the sublevel set of the $L^\infty$  norm of $\ell$. We can approximate $K$ by the intersection of the plane with unit balls in the $L^{2k}$ norm, which are rounded bodies limiting to the hypercube. Explicitly, define the $k^{\text{th}}$ approximate  $K_k$ as the sublevel set of the $L^{2k}$ norm, $K_k = \{\vert\ell\vert_{L^{2k}}\leq 1\}$. These fill out $K$ in the limit, so $\lim_{k\to \infty}K_k = K$. Moving the power $k$ to $\ell$, this becomes the intersection of an embedded submanifold with a unit ball

$$\ell^k = (\ell_1^k,\cdots,\ell_N^k) : \RR^n \to \RR^N \qquad K_k = \ell^k(\RR^n) \cap B^N(\pi).$$

As $k\to \infty$, $K_k$ fills out the corners of $K$. Try it yourself!

> The $L^{2k}$ approximations $K_k$ of $K$.

Here is an interactive plot of the embedding for $n = 2$, $N=3$. Define a symmetric hexagon on the left, and observe the image of $\ell^k$ on the right. 

> [!sketch] mahler conjecture/Lp bounds/embedding.html
> The embedding $\ell^k$. 

Notice that the result is always a cone over zero.  As $k \to \infty$, the cone limits to the coordinate axes. Also, $\ell^k$ scrunches the level sets near zero. As $k\to \infty$, the pre-image of any ball around the origin fills out all of $K_k$. 
### A first guess at a holomorphic submanifold
The real analytic map $\ell^k:\RR^n \to \RR^N$ naturally upgrades into a holomorphic map $\ell^k:\CC^n \to \CC^N$.  Define the linear functional $\ell_i: \CC^n\to \CC^n$ by $\ell_i(z) = \langle z,b_i\rangle$, and define the holomorphic map with the same formula

$$\ell^k(z) = (\ell_1(z)^k, \dots, \ell_N(z)^k)$$

Pulling back the unit ball by this map gives a domain $\Omega_k \subset \CC^n$, defined by $\Omega_k = \ell^k(\CC^n)\cap B^N(\pi)$. Matching the definitions, the intersection with the real plane is  $\Omega_k\cap \RR^n = K_k$. Here's what the domain $\Omega_k$ looks like, sliced in the imaginary direction.


> [!sketch|500] mahler conjecture/hexagonal grid/approximate.html
> Visualization of $\Omega_k$ as a family of slices over the real plane. 


The limit $\lim_{k \to \infty}\Omega_k = \Omega$  is the intersection of complex tubes, $\Omega = \bigcap_{i=1}^N \{\vert\ell_i(z)\vert^2\leq 1\}$. We see in the program that  the limiting slices $\Omega^x = \Omega \cap x+i\RR^n$ are polyhedra. Their facets are parallel to the facets of $K$, but they shrink as $x$ moves to the boundary of $K$. 

We will build our holomorphic map by modifying $\ell^k$. The domain $\Omega$ is our first attempt at building $K\times K^\circ$, which gets the real part right. The imaginary part is wrong. For one, the fibers look more like $K$ than $K^\circ$. More pressingly, the symplectic form is wrong. We endowed $\Omega_k$ with the pullback symplectic form

$${\ell^k}^\ast\omega_{std} =\frac{1}{4} \de \de^c  \sum\limits \vert\ell_i\vert^{2k},$$

which is not symplectomorphic to $\omega_{std}$. The first step is to straighten out this form, while only moving in the imaginary direction. We don't want to wreck the real intersection. Only then can we compare to $K\times K^\circ$. The solution for general $K$ is built on the $n=1$ case. 
### Building $K\times K^\circ$ when $n=1$
In dimension 1, the only convex body is a line segment. Fix the segment $K = [-1,1]$, with polar $K^\circ = [-1,1]$. Our goal is to find a holomorphic function $f:\CC \to \CC$ such that $(\{\vert f(z)\vert^2\leq 1\}, \frac{1}{4}\de \de^c \vert f\vert^2 )$ is symplectomorphic to the rectangle 

$$\square = K \times \frac{k \pi}{4} K^\circ =  [-1,1]\times \frac{k \pi}{4} [-1,1] $$

Let's see how close the functions $\ell^k = z^k$ are.  The sublevel set is the unit disc $\mathbb{D} = \{\vert z\vert^{2k}\leq 1\}$.
Denote the pullback symplectic form by $\omega_k^{\mathbb{D}} = (\ell^k)^\ast\omega$, where the superscript $\mathbb{D}$ remembers the domain of $\omega_k^{\mathbb{D}}$. This is a scaling of the standard symplectic form by a density function $\nu_k$.

$$\omega_k^{\mathbb{D}} = \frac{1}{4}\de\de^c \vert z^k\vert^2:= \nu^{\mathbb{D}}_k(z) \omega_{std}$$

> [!sketch|square] mahler conjecture/radial density/disc.html
> Plot of the normalized density function $\nu_k^\mathbb{D}/k$ on the unit disc. 

Notice as you increase $k$, the density concentrates near the boundary.

Now apply a symplectomorphism that pulls $\omega_k^{\mathbb{D}}$ back to the standard symplectic form (flag: pulls back or pushes forward?), and see how it deforms the unit disc. As discussed earlier, we will take the symplectomorphism to only change the imaginary coordinate (symplectic geometry in 2D is very flexible).  Define the accumulated density

$$J^{\mathbb{D}}_k(x+iy) = \int_{t=0}^{y} \nu^{\mathbb{D}}_k(x+it)\de t.$$

Consider the map $\Psi^{\mathbb{D}}_k: x+iy \mapsto x + iJ_k^{\mathbb{D}}(x+iy)$, which sets the imaginary part to $J^{\mathbb{D}}_k$. This is our symplectic straightening. The map $\Psi^{\mathbb{D}}_k$ only changes the vertical component, and has Jacobian $\det D\Psi_k^{\mathbb{D}} = \del_y J^{\mathbb{D}} = \nu^{\mathbb{D}}_k(z)$. Therefore, ${\Psi^{\mathbb{D}}_k}^\ast  \omega_{std} = \nu_k^{\mathbb{D}}\omega_{std} = \omega^{\mathbb{D}}_k$. This proves $(\mathbb{D}, \omega^{\mathbb{D}}_k)$ is symplectomorphic to $(\Psi^{\mathbb{D}}_k(\mathbb{D}), \omega_{std})$. Here's the result. 

> [!sketch|square] mahler conjecture/radial density/disc-map.html
> Plot of the map $\Psi^{\mathbb{D}}_k$ rescaled vertically by $1/k$. This map satisfies ${\Psi_k^\mathbb{D}}^\ast \omega_{std} = \omega_k^{\mathbb{D}}$. The slider $t$ interpolates between the identity and $\Psi^{\mathbb{D}}_k$.

The area of $\Psi_k^{\mathbb{D}}(\mathbb{D})$ is equal to $\int_{\mathbb{D}}\omega_k^{\mathbb{D}}= \pi k$, since $\omega_k^{\mathbb{D}}$ is the pullback of $\omega_{std}$ under the $k$-to-1 map $z\mapsto z^k$.  We only draw the picture rescaled in the imaginary direction by $\frac{1}{k}$. Still, things go very wrong.  The height of the boundary of $\Psi_k^{\mathbb{D}}(\mathbb{D})$ at real slice $x$ is given by $J_k^\mathbb{D}$ at the boundary, which is the integral of $\nu_k$ over the entire imaginary slice of $\Omega$. Therefore, $\Psi_k^{\mathbb{D}}(\mathbb{D})$ is a graph of the pushforward of the density of $\omega_k^\mathbb{D}$ to the real axis.  As the density concentrates near the boundary, the pushforward acquires caustics at $\pm1$, where the circle is tangent to the imaginary direction, which is the source of the large pointy ears in the plot. We will modify the construction by composing with a conformal transform, engineered to remove these caustics and force the image to be a square.

### The magic map $g$
Let $\Omega\subset \CC$ be a simply connected compact domain, symmetric about the $x$ axis, with  $g^\Omega: \Omega \to \mathbb{D}$ the conformal map to the unit disc. Define $f^\Omega_k(z) = g^\Omega(z)^k$, so the domain $\Omega$ equals the sublevel set $\{\vert f^\Omega_k(z)\vert^2 < 1\}$.  Define as before the pullback form  $\omega^\Omega_k =  {f^\Omega_k}^\ast \omega_{std} = \frac{1}{4} \de \de^c \vert f^\Omega_k\vert ^2$, the density function $\nu^\Omega_k$, the vertically integrated density $J^\Omega_k$ and the symplectomorphism $\Psi_k^\Omega$. Our domain of interest is symplectomorphic to a domain in the complex plane $\Psi_k^\Omega(\Omega)$.

$$(\{\vert f^\Omega_k\vert ^2 < 1\}, \frac{1}{4}\de \de^c \vert f^\Omega_k\vert ^2) = (\Omega, \omega_k^\Omega) \cong (\Psi^\Omega_k(\Omega),\omega_{std})$$

We will now show the lens domain $L$ from [[#^thm-holomorphic]] has a straightened domain $\Psi_k^L(L)$ that converges to the rectangle  $\square = [-1,1]\times \frac{k \pi}{4} [-1,1]$.  To do this, we compute the straightening of an arbitrary domain $\Omega$ through the limiting area measure $\lim_{k\to\infty} \omega_k^\Omega$. The form $\omega_k^\Omega$ is induced from the unit disc form $\omega_k^{\mathbb{D}}$

$$\omega_k^\Omega = \frac{1}{4}\de \de^c \vert g^\Omega(z)^k\vert ^2 = {g^\Omega}^\ast \frac{1}{4}\de \de^c \vert z\vert ^{2k} = {g^\Omega}^\ast \omega_k^\mathbb{D}$$

As $k \to \infty$, $\omega_k^\mathbb{D}$ converges to the uniform measure on the boundary. Thus, the limit $\lim_{k \to \infty} {g^\Omega}^\ast \omega_k^\mathbb{D}$  is the pullback of the uniform measure, which is called the [Harmonic measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic_measure). The harmonic measure assigns to $A \subset \del \Omega$ the length of $g^\Omega(A) \subset \del \mathbb{D}$. 

Just like the disc, the boundary of $\Psi_k^{\Omega}(\Omega)$ is the graph of the pushforward of $\omega_k^\Omega$ to the real axis. In the limit, $\lim_{k \to \infty}\Psi_k^{\Omega}(\Omega)$ is bounded by the graph of the pushforward of the harmonic measure $\lim_{k \to \infty} \omega_k^\Omega$. The domain that gives the uniform distribution is the lens domain $L$ defined in the statement of [[#^thm-holomorphic]]. To summarize, we have a $k$-to-1 symplectic map from the lens $L$ to the disc $\mathbb{D}$, and a symplectic map from the lens $L$ to the rectangle $\square$. 

$$(\square,\omega_{std}) \overset{\Psi_k\inv}{\to} (L,\omega_k) \overset{f_k}{\to}(\mathbb{D},\omega_{std})$$

The symplectic ball constructed in [[#^thm-monotonicity]] fills a sublevel set $\vert f_k\vert ^2\leq 1$ in the disc, and the pullback under $f_k\circ \Psi_k\inv$ is a ball of symplectic width $k\pi$ fully filling $\square$.  What does this composition $f_k\circ \Psi_k\inv$ look like?

>

## Visualizing the symplectic balls in $K\times K^\circ$


All together, we have produced a symplectic embedding of a ball $B^n(4)$ into $K\times K^\circ$, proving that $\Vol(K\times K^\circ)\geq \frac{4^n}{n!}$.  When is our construction sharp? Ideally, we would be able to characterize the convex bodies $K$ where $\Vol(K\times K^\circ)= \frac{4^n}{n!}$ (Conjecturally, the [Hanner polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanner_polytope)). To produce a ball, we used the monotonicity formula on a holomorphic submanifold passing through the origin. This submanifold is always conical, meaning the ball fully fills the intersection of the submanifold with the unit ball. That implies that the limit shape $\Omega_\infty$ is fully filled by the symplectic ball. What does this symplectic ball look like? 

We analyze the ball embedding fiber by fiber. We saw that as long as a fiber of the lens intersection $\Omega_{\infty}^x$ touching the lens cylinder $\vert g(\ell_i)\vert = 1$, then the fiber of the ball $D_\infty^x$ includes $b_i$. In order for $D_\infty^x = K^\circ$, it must include every vector $b_i$. If a fiber $\Omega_{\infty}^x$ doesn't intersect every lens cylinder, then $D_\infty^x$ is a convex hull of some subset of $b_i$. See the picture for an example.

 > [!figure] 
> ![](sketch/mahler conjecture/images/combined.png)
> **Left**: Slices of $\Omega_\infty^x$. Near the corner, the hexagon slices become parallelograms, then dwindle to nothing. **Right:** Slices of $D_\infty^x$. When the slices of  $\Omega_\infty^x$ lost an edge, $D_{\infty}^x$ lost a vertex. Everywhere, $D_\infty^x$ is a convex hull of a subset of $b_i$. 

Explicitly, let $V:[-1,1]\to \RR$ denote the height profile of the lens. The fiber $\Omega_{\infty}^x$ is the intersection of strips

$$\Omega_{\infty}^x = \bigcap \{\vert\langle b_i,y\rangle \vert \leq V(\langle b_i,x\rangle)\} $$

To ensure all strips are used, we take the polar and ask that all vertices are used. This requires all the vectors $b_i/ V(\langle b_i,x\rangle)$ to lie on the boundary of the convex hull. Moreover, the symplectic balls fully fill $K\times K^\circ$ only when $D_\infty^x = K^\circ$. This lets us characterize the equality case of the Mahler volume. 

> [!lemma] Characterizing equality
> The fiber $D_\infty^x$ of the symplectic ball is the convex hull of all vectors $b_i$ such that $e_i = b_i/ V(\langle b_i,x\rangle)$  is extremal on the convex hull of $\{e_i\}$.   A convex body $K$ satisfies $\Vol(K \times K^\circ) = \frac{4^n}{n!}$ if and only if $D_\infty^x = K^\circ$ for all $x$.  

In the companion paper [The symmetric Mahler conjecture and its equality cases](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/The-symmetric-Mahler-conjecture-and-its-equality-cases-September-22-2026/paper.pdf), AI (presumably) proves the only $K$ satisfying these hypotheses are [Hanner polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanner_polytope).
