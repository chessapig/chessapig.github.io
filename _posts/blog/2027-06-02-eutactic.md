A *Star* in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is a collection of vectors and their negations $\pm v_i$. We say the size of the star is the number of vectors $v_i$.  A [Eutactic star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutactic_star)  is a star such that the vectors $v_i$ are orthogonal projections of a standard orthonormal basis $e_i$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^2$. Specializing to size 3, the star $\{\pm v_1,\pm v_2, \pm v_3\}$ is eutactic if there exists an orthogonal projection matrix $P:\mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ such that $v_i = Pe_i$.  

In the above language, A drawing of a coordinate axes is equivalent to a size 3 star. The drawing is correct in orthographic perspective if this star is eutactic. To determine the correct drawings, we ask

**Question:** When is a star $\{\pm v_1,\pm v_2, \pm v_3\} \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ eutactic? 

Hadwiger's principal theorem gives us a nice answer in arbitrary dimensions

**Theorem:** A star $\pm v_i \subset ^n$ is eutactic if and only if $v_i$ form a *tight frame*, meaning $$\sum_i | v_i \rangle \langle v_i | = I$$
Before I give the proof, we need some intuition about tight frames. The bra-ket notation $| v_i \rangle \langle v_i|$ denotes the outer product of $v$ with itself. Said differently, this is the $n \times n$ matrix $v_i v_i^T$: 
$$|v\rangle\langle v| = \begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_n \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} v_1 & v_2 & \dots & v_n \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} v_1 v_1 & v_1 v_2 & \dots & v_1 v_n \\ v_2 v_1 & v_2v_2 & \dots & v_2 v_n \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ v_n v_1 & v_n v_2 & \dots & v_nv_n \end{pmatrix}$$
Geometrically, $| v \rangle \langle v|$ orthogonally projects a vector onto the 1 dimensional linear span of $v$, then scales that vector by $|v|^2$. For a tight frame, these projectors sum to the identity.

The simplest tight frame is an orthonormal basis $e_i$. Indeed, we can write any vector as a linear combination of $e_i$ with coefficient given by the dot product of the vector with $e_i$: $$w =  \sum (e_i \cdot w) e_i = \sum |e_i\rangle \langle e_i|  w\rangle $$Any tight frame satisfies this same relation.
$$w = \sum |v_i\rangle \langle v_i | w \rangle  = \sum (v_i\cdot w) v_i $$So $v_i$ must be a spanning set, and the number of $v_i$ must exceed the dimension.  I like to think of tight frames as a linear-algebraic partition of unity.

**Proof Of Hadwinger's principle theorem**
We start by showing that eutactic stars are tight frames. Suppose that $P:\mathbb{R}^n \to V$ is a projection matrix. In $\mathbb{R}^n$ we have $\sum |e_i\rangle \langle e_i | = I^n$. Sandwich each side with the projection matrix to obtain an operator from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^k$
$$\begin{align}
&P\sum_i |e_i\rangle \langle e_i |P = PI^n P\\
\implies & \sum_i |Pe_i\rangle \langle Pe_i| = P
\end{align}$$The projector $P$ acts as the identity on $\mathbb{R}^k$, so the vectors $v_i = Pe_i$ form a tight frame in $\mathbb{R}^k$.

To show every tight frame is a eutactic star, we start with a tight frame $v_i$ of $\mathbb{R}^k$ and construct an orthonormal frame $e_i$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$ such that $Pe_i = v_i$.  We start by reinterpreting the tightness condition. Form the matrix $U$ with columns given by $v_i$, of size $k\times n$: 
$$U = \begin{pmatrix}
 | &  & |\\
  v_1 & \dots  & v_n \\
| &  &  |
\end{pmatrix}$$
Tightness of $\{v_i\}$ is equivelent to the *rows* being orthonormal, meaning $UU^T = I_n$. To see this, denote the row vectors by $u_1,\dots,u_k$. By definition, $u_i^j = v_j^i = \langle v_j | e_i\rangle$. The row vectors arise when evaluating the resolution of identity on basis vectors$$\begin{align}
& \langle e_j | \left( \sum_k  |v_i\rangle \langle v_i|\right) |e_k\rangle = \langle e_j | I_n |e_k\rangle \\
\implies &\sum_k \langle e_j |v_i\rangle \langle v_i|e_k\rangle = \delta_{jk} \\ 
\implies & \sum_k u_j^i u_k^i = u_j \cdot u_k = \delta_{jk}  
\end{align}$$Since the row vectors are orthonormal, we can use gram-schmidt to continue them to an orthonormal basis. call the new vectors $u_{k+1},\dots,u_n$. use these to fill out $U$ into a square matrix, denoted by $O$.$$O = \begin{pmatrix}
 | &  & |\\
  v_1 & \dots  & v_n \\
| &  &  | \\
\text{—} & u_{k+1} & \text{—} \\
& \vdots &\\
\text{—} & u_n & \text{—}
\end{pmatrix}$$By construction, the rows of $O$ form an orthonormal basis. Therefore $O$ is orthogonal, so its columns are orthonormal. This is the desired orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$ whose projection to $\mathbb{R}^k$ yeilds $e_n$. **QED**

This theorem lets us analytically check if a given set of vectors is a eutactic star, but I find the geometry somewhat obtuse. When the star lives in $\mathbb{R}^2$, we can make the condition more explicit.  If $v = (a,b)$, the projector matrix is  $$|v\rangle\langle v| = \begin{pmatrix} a^2 & ab \\ ab & b^2 \end{pmatrix}$$If the frame $v_i$ satisfies $\sum |v_i \rangle \langle v_i| = I$, then the projection of $\sum |v_i \rangle \langle v_i|$ to the orthogonal complement of $I$ must vanish. This projection is given by the trace free part, subtracting off a multiple of the identity matrix. For each projector, the trace free part $|v_i \rangle \langle v_i|_0$ is $$|v\rangle\langle v|_0 = \begin{pmatrix} a^2 & ab \\ ab & b^2 \end{pmatrix}- \frac{1}{2}(a^2 + b^2) I = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{a^2-b^2}{2} & ab \\ ab & -\frac{a^2-b^2}{2} \end{pmatrix}$$Writing $v_i = (a_i,b_i)$, the vanishing of the trace free part imposes a relation on the $a_i$ and $b_i$.$$\sum |v_i\rangle \langle v_i|_0 = 0 \implies \sum \left(\frac{a_i^2-b_i^2}{2}, a_ib_i\right)$$Geometrically, trace zero symmetric matrices in $\mathbb{R}^2$ form a 2 dimensional vector space. For a tight frame $v_i$, each vector $v_i$ defines a vector $|v_i\rangle \langle v_i|_0$ in this two dimensional space, whose sum gives zero. Walking along these vectors tip to tail, this defines a closed polygon in 2D. *Every eutactic star defines a two dimensional polygon*, whose edges are defined from the frame vectors by $$v = (a,b) \mapsto \frac{1}{2}(a^2 - b^2, 2ab)$$The universe smiles upon us, because I recognize this formula! If we interpret $v$ as a complex number $z = a+bi$, then $z^2 = (a^2 - b^2) + (2ab) i$. The map from $v$ to its trace free projection matrix is complex squaring! The contorted seed of life is the core of what I call *cursed geometry*. If we believe the symmetries of the seed of life are divine providence, then the non-symmetric contorted seed must be perverted. The geometric rigidity of the original is replaced with indecent flexibility in the contorted seed. Far from sacred geometry, the contorted seed is cursed. Yet, the petals close up. ## petals of the contorted seed

Now we explain the miracle, and prove that the petal circles close up like we saw above. First, let's frame the problem more conveniently. We say that points $A,B,C$ are *cocircular* if there is a unit circle passing through $A,B,C$. The fact that the petals close up is equivalent to the following:

The shape formed by the radii look a whole lot like a cube, viewed corner-on. What if we include the back corner of the cube, as if drawing a wireframe? Since every edge in the cube is length one, each of the there quadrilaterals formed in the figure are in fact parallelograms. By the transitive property of parallel lines, the opposite edges of the hexagon must be parallel. So, the opposite angles must also be equal.  Therefore, we can translate the bottom right parallelogram to nestle into the top left corner. This is like drawing the back right square in the wireframe cube.   Repeating this for the other two corners, we construct the figure below.  The new edges produced from this construction are parallel to the sides of the hexagon, so are forced to have unit length.

This configuration of circles are known as the [Johnson circles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnson_circles), and the theorem above is usually called Johnson's theorem (1916). Johnson was surprised that such a simple result was new. The result, of course, was not new. The problem was posed by Hungarian mathematician Gheorghe Titeica, who stumbled upon the result while drawing circles with the 5-lei Romanian coin (A pointless compass construction!) Sometimes, this problem is called the 5-lei coin problem. An equivalent question was posed in the *mathematical repository* in 1819, see [Orthocenter and Three Equal Circles](https://www.cut-the-knot.org/Curriculum/Geometry/EqualCirclesOrthocenter.shtml#solution). A solution was submitted by a "mysterious Lady", who turned out to be [Mary Somerville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Somerville). The solution using the cube appeared before in books by Ploya and Honsburger, see the discussion and references [here](https://www.cut-the-knot.org/proofs/3circles.shtml#solution)

The key to the proof was the insight that the we can swap the central vertex of the cube from the front to the back. While the front vertex is unit distance to the centers of the petal circles, the back vertex is unit distance to $A,B,C$. This suggests a duality between the intersection points of the petal circles $A,B,C$ and the centers of the petal circles. If we temporarily forego the pointless compass with a pointed one, we can construct unit circles centered at $A,B,C$.

## Beyond the second layer...

What if we tried to go farther? Build another layer of the flower, expand out and out? If you try to do this with circles, it quickly becomes untennable. Instead, we change perspectives, and think of the contorted seed of life through its triangle $ABC$. After all, any triangle can be scaled to be the triangle of a seed of life with unit radius. This is the perspective of the [Johnson triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnson_circles). The center of triple intersection, it turns out, is the intersection of the altitudes of the triangle, also known as the orthocenter.

  <img src="/assets/images/pointless/orthocenter.jpg" alt="the lines connecting the three double intersections with the single triple intersections form the altitudes of the triangles connecting the three double intersections" class="card-img-top" >
</div>

To extend the seed of life in the direction of $B$ and $C$, we reflect the figure around $BC$.  The new triple intersection $O'$ is the reflection of $O$, and the new double intersection point $A'$ is the reflection of $A$.

<div class="card my-4 shadow-sm" style="max-width: 400px; margin: auto;">
  <img src="/assets/images/pointless/triangles.jpg" alt="underlying triangles of the circles of the contorted seed. Expanding the seed by one layer reflects the triangle." class="card-img-top" >
</div>

So to extend the seed, layer by layer, we take our original triangle and repeatedly reflect it across its own edges. If we do this with a equilateral triangle (as in the original seed of life) we get a hexagonal grid. A contorted seed blooms into a horrifying yet beautiful cacophony of triangles. I suspect that, if the original triangle has irrational angles, then the points constructed from the full contorted flower of life will be *Dense* in the plane. Yet, for some specific triangles, the contorted flower wraps back into symmetry. Try to find one of these special triangles. This same repeated unfolding construction arises in the study of triangular billiards. The question is, if you move in one direction, unfolding every time you hit an edge, does your triangle ever return back to a translate of its initial state? Equivalently, does every triangular billiards table have a periodic billiards shot? This question is wide open for irrational triangles with obtuse angles, despite being studied for more than 400 years. That should give an inkling of the complexity hiding in the fully bloomed contorted flower of life.
