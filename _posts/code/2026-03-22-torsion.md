This applet shows the torsion points of an elliptic curve sitting inside of $\mathbb{CP}^2$. 

The left pane shows the uniformized elliptic curve, thought of as a square with edges identified. Clicking and dragging the green dot changes the modulus of the elliptic curve. Here torsion points are simply scalings of the original lattice of the Elliptic curve. If the elliptic curve has lattice $\Lambda$, then the $n$-torsion points is the group $\lambda / n \lambda$, and there are $n^2$ such points. By changing the slider, you can change the order of the torsion points.

On the right is the image of these torsion points under an embedding of the elliptic curve into $\mathbb{CP}^2$. I mapped the points into projective space using $\theta$ functions, then projected to the plane using the norm squared of the coordinates in $\mathbb{CP}^2$. More details follow.

## Embedding into $\mathbb{CP}^2$
First I choose a somewhat canonical embedding into $\mathbb{CP}^2$ using theta functions. Theta functions are a choice of basis of section of holomorphic line bundles over an elliptic curve. Write the elliptic curve as $\mathbb{C}/\Lambda$ where $\Lambda$ is the lattice spanned by $1,\tau \in \CC$.  There is a unique degree $d$ line bundle with $d$ holomorphic sections, which we call the theta functions of "level $d$". A basis is supplied by the canonical theta functions, indexed by $m=0,\dots,d-1$

$$\theta^d_m(z) = \sum_{n\equiv m \mod d} \exp\left(2 i \pi \left(\frac{n^2 \tau}{2d} + nz\right) \right) $$

Notice that the standard theta functions arise when $m=d=1$. These functions are not invariant under translations by the lattice. But, for any $v\in \Lambda$, there is a holomorphic function $f_v$ such that

$$\frac{\theta^d_m(z+v)}{\theta^d_m(z)} = f_v(z)$$

Since $f_v(z)$ is the same function for the entire basis of canonical theta functions, we can build a well-defined map into projective space. Indeed, consider the map 

$$\Psi_m : \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{CP}^{d-1} \quad z \mapsto [\theta^d_0(z),\dots,\theta^d_{d-1}(z)]$$

Then we check this map is invariant under $\Lambda$, so produces a well defined map $\Psi_m : \mathbb{C}/\Lambda \to \mathbb{CP}^{d-1}$. For any $v\in \Lambda$, we have

$$ \begin{align}
 \Psi_m(z+v) &= [\theta^d_0(z+v),\dots,\theta^d_{d-1}(z+v)]\\
	 & =  [f_v(z)\theta^d_0(z),\dots,f_v(z)\theta^d_{d-1}(z)]\\
	 & = [\theta^d_0(z),\dots,\theta^d_{d-1}(z)] \\
	 & = \Psi_m(z)
\end{align}
$$

For this applet, I chose $d=3$ producing a map into $\mathbb{C}^2$ The image of the elliptic curve is still the zero locus of a cubic. Experimentally, the cubic is of the form $1+x^3 + y^3 + c xy$ . This is not the Weierstrass form, so I don't think my embedding agrees with the standard parametrization $z\mapsto [1,\wp(z),\wp'(z)]$.

## Projecting to the plane
Next I applied a map $\mu:\mathbb{CP}^2 \to \RR^2$ defined as

$$\mu([z_0,z_1,z_2]) =  \sum_{k=1}^{3} |z_k|^2 e^{2\pi i \frac{k}{3}}$$

Morally, this captures the norm squared map. For each point $[z_0,z_1,z_2] \in \mathbb{CP}^2$,  we get a triple of positive real numbers $[ \vert z_0\vert^2,\vert z_1\vert^2,\vert z_2\vert^2]$. This is only well defined up to overall scale, so we need to somehow fix the scale. The simplest way is to cut the positive orthant $\RR_{\geq 0}^3$ with a plane, then scale the coordinates to lie in the plane. This defines a map $\mu: \mathbb{CP}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$, whose image is a triangle. In particular, $\mu([z_0,z_1,z_2])$ is the point  $[\vert z_0\vert^2,\vert z_1\vert^,\vert z_2\vert^2]$ in barycentric coordinates of the triangle. For my applet, I placed the vertices of the triangle at the third roots of unity.

This map is known to symplectic geometers as the moment map of the canonical torus action on $\mathbb{CP}^2$.  The torus $U(1)^2$ rotates the phase of each coordinate on $\mathbb{CP}^2$, preserving the norm. So, the fibers of the moment map are exactly the orbits of this torus.
